**The short version:** most people picture going viral as a chain: you post, a friend shares, their friends share, and so on. Research on a billion shares says that almost never happens. Big reach comes from one big broadcast, and today the broadcaster is the feed. It shows your post to a small group, watches what they do, and keeps widening the circle while people react well. Your job is to win each circle.

## Stop picturing a chain

<BroadcastChain />

The study is from Twitter in 2011 and 2012, before recommendation feeds ran everything. Back then the big broadcasters were big accounts. Now a feed can make anyone's post the broadcast, which is why a post from a small account can reach far more people than follow it. It also means the feed, not your friends, decides how far you go.

## Win the first circle

Play with how the first viewers react and watch how far the post travels.

<SnowballFlow />

What the platforms have said publicly lines up with this picture:

- **TikTok:** recommendations are driven by what each viewer does, and follower count and past high-performing videos aren't direct factors.
- **Instagram:** Trial Reels are shown to people who don't follow you first. You see views, likes, comments and shares about 24 hours later, and Instagram can share it with everyone automatically if it does well in the first 72 hours.
- **YouTube:** recommendations drive more viewing than subscriptions or search, and they aren't based on your social network.

## Take more shots

Even in a huge dataset, big spreads were rare: about 1 in 4,000 posts reached 100 reshares, and the authors put true viral hits closer to one in a million. You can't schedule a hit. You can raise the odds on every post, and take more of them. The creators in our [case studies](/case-studies/) all made a lot of posts before the ones you know.
