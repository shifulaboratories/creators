**The short version:** a send is the strongest thing a viewer can do for you, because it pulls a new person into the app. Instagram says sends are "slightly more important" than watch time and likes for reaching people who don't follow you. In X's published code, copying a post's link is weighted about 28 times a like. And research is clear about what makes people share: content that fires them up, that surprises them, or that's genuinely useful.

## Pass the send test

Every post that gets sent has an answer to "who would I send this to, and what would I say?" Most fit one of four:

- **"This is so you."** It nails a feeling, a habit or a type of person. The viewer sends it to the friend it describes. Relatable comedy and niche-specific humor live here.
- **"You need this."** It solves a problem someone they know has. Recipes, scripts, settings, money moves.
- **"You have to see this."** Awe or surprise: a skill, a reveal, a fact that flips what you thought.
- **"Can you believe this?"** Outrage or worry on someone's behalf: a scam exposed, an unfair rule. Powerful, and risky (see below).

If you can't fill in the friend and the sentence, the post can still do well, but it's relying on the other three steps.

The best study on this looked at nearly 7,000 New York Times articles and which ones made the most-emailed list. Happy beat sad, but the bigger factor was how *activated* a piece made people feel. Tap a feeling:

## Don't buy shares with anger

Anger travels, but it also earns the actions feeds punish hardest. In X's code a report is weighted −234, against 0.708 for a like, and blocks and mutes are heavily negative too. Instagram's Explore formula subtracts the chance someone taps "see less." Make people angry *for* your audience, never *at* them, and always give them something to do with the feeling.

<DeepDive title="What the research did and didn't find" hint="Read before quoting it">

- **What it measured:** whether each of 6,956 New York Times home page articles (Aug 30 to Nov 30, 2008) made the paper's most-emailed list. Coders and software rated each article's emotion.
- **What it found:** positive content was more viral than negative, but high-arousal emotions (awe, anger, anxiety) made content more viral whatever their direction, and sadness made it less viral. One standard deviation more anger raised the odds of making the list by 34%, awe by 30%. Practically useful, interesting and surprising articles were also shared more.
- **Lab follow-ups:** the authors also ran experiments where they changed how activated people felt, and sharing moved with it.
- **What it can't tell you:** these were news articles shared by email in 2008, not videos on a feed. The direction of the findings has held up widely, but the exact percentages won't carry over to your Reels.
- **The four send reasons** above are our framework, not the paper's. The "useful," "surprise" and emotion parts are backed by it; "this is so you" is creator-tested.
