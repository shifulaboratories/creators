A video with a million views can sell almost nothing. That isn’t bad luck. Most of those viewers saw you once, for a few seconds, and then the feed moved on. Advertisers learned this decades ago: people need to run into you a few times before you stick, and most of them aren’t ready to buy on the day they first see you.

## Expect it to take three looks, not one

In 1972, Herbert Krugman, a researcher at General Electric, argued there are three kinds of exposure: curiosity, recognition and decision. The first makes people curious (“what is this?”). The second, they recognize it (“oh, this again”) and weigh whether it’s for them. The third is where they decide. In his words, there is “no such thing as a fourth exposure psychologically”: everything after repeats the third.

In 1979 the Association of National Advertisers reviewed the published studies. Its conclusions: one exposure “has little or no effect” in all but a minority of cases, and two to three exposures within a purchase cycle is the level where advertising works.

You’ll hear “the rule of 7” too. We couldn’t trace it to any study. The closest is a list in Thomas Smith’s 1885 book *Successful Advertising*, which walks through 20 exposures, starting with “the first time people look at any given ad, they don’t even see it.” Treat 7 as folklore and three as the research.

## See how often your audience has actually seen you

Every post reaches a slice of the people you want. Play with the numbers to see how many of them have seen you enough to remember.

<ImpressionStacker />

Two things to notice. Posting more on one account helps, but adding more **places** people can see you (a clips page, collabs, a second format) moves people from “saw you once” to “saw you enough” faster. That’s the real reason big creators run [clipping programs](/strategy/clipping/) and [fan pages](/strategy/fan-pages/). And time matters: the same plan over eight weeks reaches far more people three times than over one.

## Stay remembered for when they’re ready

Even people who know and like you usually aren’t shopping right now. Research by Prof. John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, for LinkedIn’s B2B Institute, put it at about 5% of business buyers in the market at any given time. As the authors put it, “the brand that gets remembered is the brand that gets bought.”

That study is about companies buying from companies, but the logic holds for creators: a follower who doesn’t need a planner today may need one in January. If you stop mentioning it after launch week, someone else will be the name they remember.

## What this means for you

- **Brand deals:** one post is one exposure. A three-post package, or a post plus a Story reminder, is closer to what makes ads work. That’s a good reason to [price and pitch packages](/deals/).
- **Your own products and memberships:** plan launches as a series, not a moment. Then keep a quiet mention in rotation.
- **Growth:** reach still matters. Repeat exposure only works on people you’ve reached once. The craft of getting that first look is in [our course on content that travels](/algorithms/).

<DeepDive title="What the research says, and doesn’t" hint="">

**Effective frequency is a range, not a magic number.** John Philip Jones wrote in the *Journal of Advertising Research* in 1997 that the phrase was coined to say there must be “enough concentration of media weight to cross a threshold,” not to fix one number for every product.

**It depends on the buying cycle.** The ANA review quotes media planner Erwin Ephron: reach and frequency goals “must be related to the product purchase cycle.” Something people buy weekly needs repeat exposure within the week; a laptop has a much longer cycle.

**These studies are about ads, not organic posts.** A creator’s post is usually more welcome than an ad, so it may take fewer looks. Nobody has published a study on exactly how many. Our model above is illustrative math, not platform data.
