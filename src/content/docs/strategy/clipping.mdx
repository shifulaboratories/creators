Clipping is paying other people to cut your long videos, streams or podcasts into short clips and post them on their own accounts. You pay per view, usually per 1,000. It’s why the same MrBeast, Adin Ross or Kick streamer moment shows up from ten different accounts in your feed.

It started with streamers and has spread to musicians, podcasters, brands and even a mayoral campaign. Bloomberg reported in October 2025 that MrBeast has more than a thousand paid clippers. One agency’s campaign for Adin Ross got 430 million views across 11,000 videos from 520 clippers, and the same agency has run campaigns for Ice Spice and Offset.

## See what a budget buys

Rates vary a lot. Broad campaign sites typically pay clippers $1 to $5 per 1,000 views, some as little as $0.20. MrBeast paid one agency’s clippers $50 per 100,000 views. Tap a reported rate to start from it.

## Run a campaign in five steps

Most campaigns run through a marketplace like Whop’s Content Rewards, an agency, or your own Discord. On Whop, according to its setup guide, it works like this:

<ol class="sc-steps">
<li><strong>Set a budget.</strong> The total you’ll spend. Nothing is charged until you deposit it.</li>
<li><strong>Set the rate per 1,000 views.</strong> Plus a minimum payout per clip (so tiny posts don’t clog your review queue) and a maximum (so one viral clip can’t eat the budget).</li>
<li><strong>Pick the platforms.</strong> TikTok, YouTube Shorts, Instagram Reels and X are the usual ones.</li>
<li><strong>Write the brief.</strong> What to clip, what not to, and the rules (below).</li>
<li><strong>Approve each post.</strong> Clippers submit links; you approve or reject. Whop pays on views only after you approve.</li>
</ol>

## Know when it works, and when it doesn’t

Clipping multiplies what you already have. It works best for creators with lots of long-form material and moments that land without context: streamers, podcasters, long YouTube videos, live shows. One gaming exec told Digiday it can be “a bit prohibitive” for creators who aren’t already popular. A Vulture critic, writing about a band’s campaign, put it more bluntly: clipping isn’t a magic bullet, and you still need a real audience.

It also feeds the [repeat-exposure effect](/strategy/multiple-impressions/): ten clip pages showing the same people your best moments is ten more chances to be remembered.

The risks are real. Digiday’s critics say clipping rewards “outrage, volume and manipulation over substance.” Wikipedia’s summary of the reporting notes claims that clip views can be botted. And in April 2026 Instagram head Adam Mosseri said original creators should be rewarded, “not compete with accounts that simply re-upload it.”

## If you want to clip for money

Clipping is a real side income, and some clippers build editing careers from it. Expect a few dollars per 1,000 views at most, payment only after approval, and campaigns that end when the budget runs out. The originality rules apply to you, not just the creator: re-uploads earn nothing on platforms that won’t recommend them. Disclose every paid clip.

<DeepDive title="The rules that apply to clips" hint="">

**Disclosure (FTC).** A per-view payment is a financial relationship. The FTC says to disclose it in the post or video itself (“not just in the description”), with clear words like “ad” or “sponsored,” not “sp” or “collab.” Digiday notes brands that don’t disclose paid clips can break both platform terms and FTC rules. A lawyer it interviewed added that brand clipping raises publicity-rights questions when a person’s likeness promotes a product.

**Instagram.** When two identical posts exist, only the original is recommended, and accounts that repost others’ content 10 or more times in 30 days lose recommendations. In 2026 this extended to photos and carousels, and adding a watermark or changing speed doesn’t count as an edit.

**TikTok.** Unoriginal content “imported or uploaded without any new or creative edits,” including someone else’s visible watermark, isn’t eligible for For You.

**YouTube.** Clips and compilations aren’t monetized unless they’re changed “significantly to make it your own,” such as adding a storyline and commentary. The policy applies “even if you have permission from the original creator,” and to the whole channel.
