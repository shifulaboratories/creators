**The short version:** an **LLC** is a legal wrapper. It can help separate your business from your personal stuff, but by itself it **doesn't change your federal taxes at all**. An **S corp** is a tax election. It can lower self-employment tax once your profit is high enough, but it adds payroll, a second tax return and real costs. A common path: start as a sole proprietor, add an LLC when deals and contracts get serious, and look at an S corp only once profit is steady. <span class="sc-tag is-practitioner">Practitioner view</span>

:::caution[Not tax or legal advice]
Entity choice depends on your state, your income and your plans. Talk to a CPA, and for liability questions a business lawyer, before you form anything or file an election.
:::

## The three setups, side by side

| | Sole proprietor | Single-member LLC | LLC or corp taxed as an S corp |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **What it is** | You, doing business. The default the day you get paid | A company you register with your state | A tax election you file with the IRS (Form 2553) |
| **Federal tax** | Schedule C + self-employment tax on all profit | **Same as sole proprietor** by default. The IRS calls it a "disregarded entity" | Salary through payroll; the rest can come out as distributions without self-employment tax |
| **Liability** | No separation | Can help shield personal assets, if you keep finances separate | Same as the LLC or corporation underneath |
| **Paperwork** | Least | State filing and yearly state fees | Payroll, a separate business tax return, more bookkeeping |
| **Brands see** | Your name and SSN or EIN on a W-9 | The LLC's name and EIN | The company's name and EIN |

## What an LLC is good for

- **A clean line** between business and personal money, which also makes bookkeeping easier.
- **Contracts in the company's name**, so brands sign with "Your Studio LLC," not you personally.
- **Some liability protection.** It's not a force field: it can fail if you mix personal and business money, and it doesn't protect you from your own mistakes. A lawyer can tell you how much it really covers in your state.

**What it costs:** state formation and yearly fees, which vary a lot. California, for example, charges every LLC that's organized or doing business there an **$800** annual tax, plus an extra fee once California income passes $250,000. <span class="sc-tag is-platform">CA Franchise Tax Board</span>

**Get an EIN** (a business tax ID) once the LLC exists. It's free from the IRS website and takes minutes. The IRS warns: "Beware of websites that charge for an EIN. You never have to pay a fee for an EIN." Putting an EIN on your W-9s also keeps your Social Security number out of brands' inboxes.

## How an S corp saves money

As a sole proprietor or default LLC, all of your profit gets the 15.3% self-employment tax. With an S corp, you pay yourself a salary through payroll (which gets the same Social Security and Medicare taxes), and the profit above that can be paid out as distributions, which don't.

The catch is the word **reasonable.** The IRS says S corps "must pay reasonable compensation to a shareholder-employee in return for services... before non-wage distributions may be made." When the money comes from *your own* work, as it does for most creators, the IRS looks hard at a low salary and can reclassify distributions as wages. It lists factors like your training, duties, time spent, and what comparable businesses pay for similar work. There's no official percentage.

## Timing

To have an S corp election start on January 1, a calendar-year business generally files Form 2553 **no more than 2 months and 15 days into the year** (around March 15), or any time in the year before. Late elections can sometimes be fixed within 3 years and 75 days. <span class="sc-tag is-platform">IRS</span>

## Questions to bring to a CPA

- "What would a reasonable salary be for what I do, and how would you document it?"
- "What will payroll, the extra return and my state's fees cost me each year?"
- "Does my state tax S corps or LLCs on top of federal tax?"
- "How does this change my QBI deduction and retirement contributions?"
- "If my income drops next year, how hard is it to undo?"
