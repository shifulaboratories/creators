**The short version:** in the US, creator income is business income. You pay two federal taxes on your profit: **income tax**, like everyone, and **self-employment tax**, which covers your Social Security and Medicare because no employer is paying half. Most states add their own income tax. Nothing is taken out of a brand payment or a platform payout, so you're the one who has to put it aside.

:::caution[Not tax advice]
General US federal rules for tax year 2026, checked November 26, 2026. Talk to a CPA or enrolled agent about your own return.
:::

Notice that self-employment tax is the bigger bill here. That surprises most people the first year, and it's the tax an [S corp election](/money/llc-vs-s-corp/) can shrink once profit is high enough.

## 1099s: the forms, not the rule

Brands and platforms send you tax forms that report what they paid you. They're useful for checking your records, but **you owe tax on all of your income whether or not a form shows up.** The IRS says this in so many words.

| Form | Who sends it | When, for 2026 payments |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1099-NEC** | A brand or agency that paid you for work | If they paid you $2,000 or more in the year (up from $600). The threshold rises with inflation from 2027 |
| **1099-K** | Payment apps and marketplaces (PayPal, Venmo for business, Stripe and similar) | Over $20,000 **and** over 200 payments in the year. Some states set a lower bar, and an app may send one anyway |
| **1099-MISC** | Some platforms, for royalties, prizes or other payments | Thresholds depend on the kind of payment |
| **W-9** | You send this one | Brands ask for it before they pay you. It gives them your name and taxpayer ID |

<span class="sc-tag is-platform">IRS</span> Thresholds from IRS Publication 1099 (2026) and the IRS Form 1099-K FAQs.

## Gifted products and PR packages

If a brand sends you something **in exchange for a post**, that's payment in kind. The IRS treats it like bartering: "You must include in gross income in the year of receipt the fair market value of goods or services received from bartering." <span class="sc-tag is-platform">IRS</span>

- **Deal for content** (product for a post, a trip for coverage): count the product's fair market value as income.
- **Truly unsolicited, no strings**: this is less settled, and tax pros disagree on how to treat it. Keep a list of what arrived, what it's worth and whether anything was expected. Ask your CPA how they want to handle it. <span class="sc-tag is-practitioner">Practitioner view</span>
- Sending it back or giving it away the same week, with no post, makes for a much cleaner story.

## State and local tax

Most states tax your profit too, at their own rates, and a few cities do as well. Some states have no income tax at all. Rules change, so check your state revenue department's site rather than a list online. If you move mid-year, you may file in two states.

## Got a day job too?

Your creator profit stacks on top of your salary, so it gets taxed at your **top** bracket, not from the bottom. Two easy ways to cover it:

- Make quarterly payments for the creator side (see [Set aside and pay quarterly](/money/set-aside/)).
- Or ask your job to withhold more by filing a new Form W-4, so the extra tax comes out of each paycheck.

## What to ask a tax pro

- "How should I treat gifted products and trips?"
- "Do I need to file in more than one state?"
- "At what profit would an S corp start to make sense for me?"
