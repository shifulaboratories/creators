**The short version:** first check the notice is inside the app, because fake copyright notices are one of the most common creator scams. Then work out whether it costs you money (a claim) or your account (a strike), and pick the fix: wait it out, ask for a retraction, or file a counter-notification if you have the rights.

## Step zero: is it even real?

Real copyright notices appear **inside the app or YouTube Studio**. If it came as a DM, a comment, or an email with an "Appeal" button that asks you to log in, it's phishing. Open the app yourself and check. Read the [scam patterns](/protect/scams/) to see what fakes look like.

## Before you counter-notify

A counter-notification is a legal statement made under penalty of perjury, and it hands your name and address to the person who claimed. It's the right move when:

- you made it, or you have a license, and can show it, or
- your use is clearly fair use (commentary, criticism, parody) and you're comfortable defending that.

If you're unsure, ask the claimant for a retraction first, or talk to a lawyer. Under US law, the claimant then has 10 business days to show they've gone to court, or the platform can restore your content.

## Not your fault? Fake claims happen

Copyright systems act first and check later, and scammers know it. In 2026 Malwarebytes, citing BBC reporting, described people filing fake claims against Instagram creators to get them suspended, then demanding crypto to withdraw them. Meta acknowledged in court that 13 strikes against one user were fraudulent. If that's you: don't pay, appeal through the official route, keep every message, and report the extortion. <span class="sc-tag is-platform">Reported</span>
