**The short version:** a manager runs your career, an agent books your work, a lawyer protects you in contracts, and a business manager handles the money. They're paid differently, and only some of them are allowed to do each job. Before anyone takes a percentage, know which seat they're sitting in.

## Who works for whom

Commissions stack. A creator with a manager, an agent and a business manager can be giving up 30% or more of gross before paying a single editor. That can still be a great deal if each person brings in more than they cost, which is the math on [when to get a manager](/team/when-to-get-a-manager/).

## Who's allowed to do what

This is where creators get burned, so it's worth one careful read.

- **Agents need a license in California.** The state says anyone "involved in arranging employment for an artist in the entertainment field must get a license to operate as a talent agency." The license is issued by the Labor Commissioner.
- **Managers usually aren't licensed, and in California they aren't supposed to find you work.** California's Supreme Court confirmed in *Marathon v. Blasi* (2008) that the Talent Agencies Act applies to managers too. A manager who procured work without a license had their contract voided by the Labor Commissioner, though the court let lawful parts of the deal survive.
- **In practice,** many creator managers do source brand deals. Where that is legally risky depends on the state and the type of work. If it matters to you, ask a lawyer, not the manager.
- **Upfront fees to "represent" you are illegal in California.** Since 2010, Labor Code 1702 bans advance-fee talent representation. A percentage of what you actually earn is fine. A fee before you earn anything is a red flag anywhere.
- **Agents' cut is capped more often.** Union (guild) agreements limit agents to 10% for union work, and California's Labor Commissioner approves up to 20%. Managers' fees aren't regulated.

## What each one is best at

| You need… | Call a… | Why not the others |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A strategy, a team, and someone to say no to bad deals | Manager | An agent focuses on booking, a lawyer on documents |
| Bigger paid deals: TV, books, speaking, top-tier brands | Agent | Managers may not be allowed to procure work in some states |
| A contract read before you sign | Lawyer | Managers and agents are not your legal counsel, and have a stake in the deal closing |
| Taxes, payroll, bills, investments | Business manager or CPA | Your manager shouldn't hold your money |
| YouTube rights management or ad sales help | MCN, carefully | Read the [vetting checklist](/team/vetting-a-manager/) first |

:::tip[Get the contract read either way]
Even with a great manager, have your own lawyer look at the management agreement itself. The manager can't advise you on their own contract. Our [contract redline guide](/deals/redline-a-contract/) covers what to look for.
:::
