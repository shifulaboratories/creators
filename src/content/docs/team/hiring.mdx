**The short version:** hire for the job that's slowing you down the most, which for most video creators is editing. Start freelance, pay for a real trial, write down exactly what the role owns, and keep the rights to everything they make for you.

A quick test: write down everything you did last week and circle what only you can do (on camera, your ideas, your voice). Everything else is a job description.

## What to pay

There's no standard rate. Pay depends on skill, video length, turnaround and where people live. These published figures give you a sense of the range, so you can tell a fair offer from a lowball.

<div class="sc-stat is-rose"><span class="sc-stat__value">$25–$100</span><span class="sc-stat__label">Typical single thumbnail on Upwork, by Upwork's estimate</span></div>

<figcaption>US figures. Freelancers price per project; full-time hires are salaried.</figcaption>

- **Editors.** The US government's median for film and video editors is $75,420 a year, with the top 10% above $148,550 and the bottom 10% under $40,280. Indeed's job postings average $33.23 an hour, from $14.45 to $76.42. Most YouTube editors start freelance and price per video, so ask for a per-video quote and compare it to the hours involved.
- **Thumbnail designers.** Upwork estimates $25–$100 for a single thumbnail, $100–$500 for a batch of 3–10, and $200–$800 a month for ongoing channel support.
- **Ops and assistants.** Rates vary hugely by country and hours. Ask candidates for their rate, and pay the market rate where they live, not the lowest number you can find.
- **Raise people who raise you.** If an editor's work grows your views, a raise or a bonus tied to performance keeps them from being poached.

## Pay people properly

- **Put it in writing.** Scope, rate, turnaround, number of revisions, how you'll pay, and how either side can end it.
- **Own what you pay for.** Your agreement should assign you the rights to the edits, thumbnails and project files they make for you.
- **Contractor or employee?** In the US, it depends on how much control you have over how and when the work is done, not on what you call it. The IRS explains the test. Get it wrong and you can owe back taxes.
- **Tax forms.** US businesses generally file a 1099-NEC for contractors paid above the IRS threshold in a year. Your accountant or business manager should handle this.
- **Pay on time.** Your reputation travels fast in editor communities.
