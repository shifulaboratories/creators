**The short version:** you're hiring someone who will take a share of your income for years. Interview them like it. Call their current clients, read every clause about money and exit, and have your own lawyer review the contract before you sign.

## Check them out before the first call

<ol class="sc-steps">
<li><strong>Look them up</strong><span>Who do they manage now? Search their name with "lawsuit" and "owed." Check the state license if they say they're an agent.</span></li>
<li><strong>Call two current clients</strong><span>And one former client, found yourself, not handed to you. Ask what changed after signing.</span></li>
<li class="is-active"><strong>Get the contract early</strong><span>Ask for the draft before you're excited. Read the money and exit clauses first.</span></li>

**California agents are public record.** The Labor Commissioner licenses talent agencies, so you can ask for the license number and check it. Managers aren't licensed, which is why references matter more.

## Ask these on the first call

<CopyBlock
  label="Questions for the first call"
  text={`- Who else do you manage, and how many clients does each manager carry?
- What will you do in the first 90 days?
- What do you commission, and on what?
- Who collects the money?
- What happens if my manager leaves the company?
- How do I get out?`}
  note="Copy into your notes and write their answers underneath."
/>

What good answers sound like:

- **Who else do you manage, and how many clients does each manager carry?** A manager with 40 clients can't give you the same attention as one with 8.
- **What will you do in the first 90 days?** You want specifics: rate card, target brands, a plan for your next big swing.
- **What do you commission, and on what?** Gross or net? Deals they didn't touch? AdSense? Your own products?
- **Who collects the money?** Ideally brands pay you (or your business manager) and you pay commission. If they collect, ask how fast they pay out and whether it sits in a separate client account.
- **What happens if my manager leaves the company?** Ask for a key-person clause so you can leave too.
- **How do I get out?** If the answer is vague, so is your exit.

## Read the contract clause by clause

| Clause | What's common | What to push for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Commission** | 15–20% of gross, influencer managers | A lower rate on income they didn't generate. Net instead of gross if you can get it |
| **Term** | Management agreements often run 3–5 years | A shorter first term, or an exit if you miss agreed targets |
| **Post-term (sunset)** | Commission continues on deals made during the term | A cap: commission that steps down and ends, for example over 1–2 years |
| **Scope** | Covers all your entertainment income | Exclude income from before signing, and businesses they don't help build |
| **Key person** | Often missing | You can leave if your named manager leaves |
| **Power of attorney** | Some contracts let the manager sign for you | Narrow it, or remove it. You approve every deal in writing |
| **Expenses** | Some managers bill travel or "admin" | Pre-approved expenses only. Pfeiffer Law says managers generally shouldn't charge an admin fee |
| **Money handling** | Varies | Payments to you or your business manager, with a monthly statement |

## Treat networks (MCNs) differently

Multi-channel networks were huge in YouTube's early years. YouTube's own help page says MCNs "typically take a percentage of revenue from the creator's portion before paying them," and that their contracts are legally binding. YouTube tells creators to check, before signing:

- the fees charged by the network,
- what services and support you actually get,
- your obligations, the contract length, and how to terminate,
- when you get paid, and "what protections you have if your MCN fails to pay you."

That last point isn't hypothetical. When Defy Media collapsed in 2018, creators said they were owed fees, an estimated $1.7 million according to Digiday's reporting. See the [agency directory](/team/agency-directory/) for what happened to the big networks.
