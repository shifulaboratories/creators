**The short version:** post at a pace you can keep for months, not the most you can manage this week. The best large dataset we found says steady weeks matter more than volume, more posts mostly raise your odds of an outlier rather than your typical views, and the exact time you post matters far less than people think.

## What the data says

Buffer, the scheduling company, has published three large studies of its users' accounts. They're not controlled experiments, and Buffer users aren't everyone, but they're the biggest public datasets on this. <span class="sc-tag is-practitioner">Large dataset</span>

**Steady weeks beat bursts.** In 100,000+ accounts over 26 weeks, people who posted in 20 or more of those weeks got more than 5 times the engagement per post of people who posted in 4 weeks or fewer. Engagement per post kept climbing until about 21 weeks of steady posting, then leveled off.

**On Instagram, more helps, with diminishing returns.** Across 2.1 million posts from 102,000 accounts, posting 3–5 times a week came with about 12% more reach per post than 1–2 times; 6–9 about 18%; 10+ about 24%. Follower growth rose the same way. Buffer's own analyst adds that "the most important factor probably remains content quality."

**On TikTok, more posts mostly buy more lottery tickets.** Across 11.4 million posts from 150,000+ accounts, median views per post barely moved with frequency. What changed was the top end: the 90th-percentile post got much bigger.

So posting more on TikTok doesn't make your typical post do better. It gives you more chances at a breakout. That's worth it only if quality holds.

## What the platforms say

YouTube's own checklist asks two questions about your schedule: how often you upload, and whether that frequency is "sustainable over the long term." It calls a "consistent, sustainable release schedule" critical for both audience expectations and your wellbeing, and suggests filming in batches. <span class="sc-tag is-platform">From the platform</span>

On timing, YouTube's Todd Beaupré has said the system cares whether viewers are satisfied, not whether you uploaded "at three in the morning or seven at night." Creator Liaison Rene Ritchie says that over a few weeks, total views usually even out regardless of posting time, and that holding a finished video for 24–48 hours before publishing makes no difference. The exception: news and trends, which you should post as soon as possible.

## Find your pace

<CadencePlanner />

The planner leaves 20% slack on purpose. Weeks go sideways, comments need replies, and a schedule with no slack is the one that breaks first. Buffer's consistency finding is about weeks you showed up, not posts per week, so the real goal is never having a zero week.

## Making it sustainable

- **Batch.** Film three or four in one session. Edit on a different day. YouTube recommends batching too.
- **Use a series.** A [repeatable format](/grow/formats/) cuts planning time in half because the structure is already decided.
- **Repurpose with care.** One idea can become five posts, if you remake each one for its feed. [How →](/grow/repurposing/)
- **Plan breaks.** Tell your audience. YouTube's checklist even asks whether you've taken breaks, as something to consider, not something to fear.
