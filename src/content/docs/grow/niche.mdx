**The short version:** a niche is a specific person you help or entertain, plus the thing you make for them, plus the angle only you bring. "Fitness" isn't a niche. "Strength training at home for nurses on night shift, from a nurse" is. Narrow feels scary, but feeds match posts to people, and a clear person is what makes the matching work.

## Write your niche in one sentence

<div class="sc-compare">
<div class="sc-compare__side is-rose"><h3>Who it's for</h3><ul><li>A life stage, job, identity or situation</li><li>Specific enough that they'd say "that's me"</li><li>Example: first-time renters</li></ul></div>
<div class="sc-compare__side is-cyan"><h3>What you make</h3><ul><li>The topic and the format</li><li>Something with 100+ ideas in it</li><li>Example: tiny-apartment decor</li></ul></div>
<div class="sc-compare__side is-champagne"><h3>Your angle</h3><ul><li>Your job, access, taste, story or constraint</li><li>The part a copycat can't take</li><li>Example: with $50 or less</li></ul></div>
</div>

The angle is where most creators under-invest. Two people can make the same topic for the same person and still feel completely different: one a dietitian with sources, one a busy mom who tests everything on her kids. Both work. Pick the one that's actually you, because you'll be making it for years.

## Small and loyal pays

You don't need millions of followers to earn a living. Kevin Kelly's 2008 essay argued 1,000 "true fans" paying $100 a year adds up to $100,000. Li Jin updated it in 2020: as creators sell more to their biggest fans, 100 true fans paying $1,000 a year gets you to the same place. She cites a course creator who made $110,000 with 76 students.

Li Jin is careful to add that the model "isn't for everyone." But it changes what a niche is for: not the biggest possible audience, but the people most likely to care. See [Earn](/earn/) for how creators turn that into income.

## Torn between two ideas?

If you're torn, don't decide in your head. Make five posts on each, the same week, and compare the boring numbers: how many people watched most of it, how many sent it, how many followed. Then pick the one you'd still want to make in a year. [How to run that test →](/grow/experiments/)

## Avoid these traps

- **Picking for money alone.** High-paying topics are crowded, and you'll quit before it pays if you don't care about it.
- **Being too clever.** If people can't tell who it's for in one second, they swipe.
- **Never narrowing.** "Lifestyle" works for people who are already famous. For everyone else, it's a polite way of saying "nobody in particular."
- **Fearing you'll be stuck.** Niches widen as you grow. Start where you can win, then expand to the next ring of people.

<DeepDive title="Why narrow grows faster" hint="">

Recommendation feeds don't show your post to "everyone." They predict, viewer by viewer, who is likely to watch, send or follow, and test it on them first. YouTube's Todd Beaupré put it simply on stage at VidCon: whenever you're tempted to say "algorithm," say "audience" instead.

A broad account sends mixed signals. If one post is a recipe and the next is a hot take on a celebrity, the people who loved the recipe don't care about the take, and the feed learns that your followers don't reliably want your next post. MrBeast's version: "If you watch 100 episodes of 'SpongeBob' and then the 101st is 'Adventure Time,' you'd be like, what is this? The same is true about your audience." [Case study →](/case-studies/mrbeast/)

A clear person also makes every other decision easier. You know what hook will make them stop, what title they'd search, and what they'd send to a friend.
